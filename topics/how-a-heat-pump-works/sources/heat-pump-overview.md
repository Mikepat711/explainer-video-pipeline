# Heat pump overview (hand-written summary notes)

Reading list used to write these notes: US Department of Energy "Heat Pump Systems" guide, IEA "The Future
of Heat Pumps" report, and introductory thermodynamics texts on the vapour-compression cycle.

Heat naturally flows from a warmer place to a colder place, never the other way on its own.
A heat pump uses mechanical work to move heat against this natural direction, from a cold space to a warm one.
Even outdoor air at zero degrees Celsius contains a large amount of thermal energy that can be extracted.
A heat pump does not create heat by burning fuel or by resistance heating; it transfers existing heat.
The working fluid is a refrigerant, a substance chosen to boil at very low temperatures at low pressure.
Common modern refrigerants include R-32, R-410A and natural refrigerants such as propane (R-290) and CO2.

The vapour-compression cycle has four main components: an evaporator, a compressor, a condenser and an expansion valve.
In the evaporator, cold low-pressure liquid refrigerant absorbs heat from the outdoor air and evaporates into a gas.
Evaporation absorbs a large amount of latent heat, which is why the refrigerant can collect heat even from cold air.
The compressor raises the pressure of the refrigerant vapour, which also raises its temperature, often to 60 degrees Celsius or more.
In the condenser, the hot high-pressure gas releases heat into the home and condenses back into a liquid.
The expansion valve then lowers the pressure abruptly, which makes the refrigerant cold again before it returns to the evaporator.
The electricity is used mainly by the compressor and the fans.

The efficiency of a heat pump is described by its coefficient of performance, or COP.
COP is the ratio of heat delivered to electrical energy consumed.
Typical air-source heat pumps achieve a COP of about 3 to 4 in mild weather, meaning 3 to 4 units of heat per unit of electricity.
COP falls as the temperature difference between outside and inside grows, but modern units still work below minus 15 degrees Celsius.
By comparison, an electric resistance heater has a COP of 1.

A reversing valve changes the direction of refrigerant flow.
In cooling mode, the indoor coil becomes the evaporator and absorbs heat from the room, while the outdoor coil rejects it outside.
This means the same heat pump can both heat and cool a building, working like an air conditioner in summer.
Ground-source heat pumps use the stable temperature of the ground instead of the outdoor air, giving higher and steadier efficiency.
