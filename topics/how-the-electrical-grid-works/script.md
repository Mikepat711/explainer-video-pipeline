## [kettle-moment] A kettle and a distant generator
You flip on an electric kettle, and it starts drawing about 1,500 watts. At that same instant, a generator hundreds of miles away has to work a little harder. Nothing was stored for you in the wires. So how does the grid know, and how does it keep up?
> concept: The grid has no stockpile of electricity, so every appliance you switch on is served by generation happening at that same moment.
> visual: A split screen shows a kitchen kettle on the left and a turbine hall far across a map on the right, linked by a thin line of wire. When the kettle switch clicks, a pulse runs down the line, and the turbine's load gauge ticks up at the same moment. An empty 'storage tank' outline on the wire stays empty to show that nothing was waiting there.

## [spinning-magnet] A magnet spinning in coils
Most grid power starts with a magnet spinning inside coils of copper wire. Steam, falling water, or wind turns a turbine, and the turbine spins that magnet. As the magnetic field sweeps past the coils, it pushes a voltage that rises, falls, and reverses. That's alternating current, swinging back and forth 60 times every second in North America. When your kettle draws current, that current pushes back on the magnet, so the turbine must push harder.
> concept: Generators make alternating current by spinning a magnet past coils of wire, and drawing current pushes back on that magnet, so delivering more power takes more push.
> visual: A cutaway shows a magnet rotor turning inside a stator coil, and a sine wave is drawn beside it in step with the rotation, crossing zero each time a pole passes. When a kettle icon switches on, curved counter-torque arrows appear on the rotor, and the steam jet driving the turbine has to grow larger to keep the rotation speed constant.

## [energy-not-electrons] Energy travels, electrons don't
The electrons from that power plant never reach your kitchen. In the wires, they just jiggle back and forth in place. The energy travels as an electromagnetic wave guided by the wires, at a large fraction of the speed of light.
> concept: In AC wiring, electrons only oscillate in place while energy races down the line as an electromagnetic wave.
> visual: Zoom into a wire where a row of electron dots shuffles left and right, each one staying within a tiny marked zone. Above them, a glowing wavefront of field lines sweeps the full length of the wire from plant to house in an instant.

## [step-up] Why transmission voltage is so high
Pushing that energy hundreds of miles has a cost: wires heat up as current flows through them. That heat grows with the square of the current. So at the plant, a transformer raises the voltage to hundreds of thousands of volts. Power is voltage times current, so the same power now flows with far less current. Double the voltage, and the heat lost drops to one quarter.
> concept: Raising the voltage lets the same power flow with less current, and because heat loss grows with the square of the current, losses fall sharply.
> visual: A long transmission line glows red-hot with a thick stream of current arrows. A transformer at the plant end turns a voltage dial upward, the current stream thins to a trickle, and the line's glow fades. A power bar labeled 'V × I' keeps the same length the whole time. A final step doubles the voltage dial, halves the current, and shrinks the heat-loss bar to one quarter.

## [step-down] Stepping down to your street
Near your town, substations step the voltage down in stages. Feeder lines carry it along the streets, and a transformer on a pole drops it to the 120 volts at your outlet.
> concept: Substations and neighborhood transformers lower the voltage in stages until it is safe for the outlets in your home.
> visual: The camera follows the line from tall transmission towers to a substation, then down to street poles and into a house. At each stop, a voltage counter rolls downward, from hundreds of thousands of volts to thousands and finally to 120 V, while the wires get shorter and lower to the ground.

## [balance-frequency] No storage, so frequency dips
Here is the catch: the grid stores almost no energy. Every watt used must be generated in the same instant. When your kettle switches on, the extra energy first comes from the spinning mass of every generator on the grid. They all slow down together, very slightly, and the frequency dips below 60 Hz.
> concept: Because the grid stores almost nothing, a sudden new demand is first paid for by the spinning mass of every generator, and they all slow together, which lowers the frequency.
> visual: A balance scale holds generation on one side and demand on the other, with a frequency needle reading 60.00 Hz at its pivot. A kettle weight drops onto the demand side, a row of linked flywheels representing the generators all slow together, and the needle sags toward 59.98 Hz.

## [control-response] Frequency is the signal
That tiny dip is the signal. Within seconds, governors on the generators and fast batteries sense it and push out more power. Over the next minutes, the control center fine-tunes output until the frequency is back at exactly 60.
> concept: Frequency acts as the grid's live gauge: automatic controls react within seconds, and operators restore the exact frequency over minutes.
> visual: A frequency trace dips below 60 Hz. When the governors' steam valves open wider and a battery's output bar jumps, the dip stops falling and levels off. A control-room dial then nudges the plants' setpoints, and the trace climbs smoothly back to 60.00 Hz, with each response marked at the moment it acts.

## [heartbeat] One machine, one heartbeat
If frequency falls too far, below about 59.3 Hz in Texas, automatic relays switch off whole neighborhoods to save the rest. So the grid isn't a warehouse of electricity. It's one enormous machine spinning in lockstep across a continent, and your kettle just nudged it.
> concept: The grid is one synchronized machine whose frequency must stay in a narrow band, and if balance can't be restored, it sheds load to save itself.
> visual: A frequency gauge shows a narrow green band around 60 Hz with a red line at 59.3 Hz. When the needle is pushed toward the red line, relays click open and a few neighborhood blocks on a map go dark, and the needle recovers for everyone else. The view then pulls back to a continent of generators, all rotating in sync like one shared pulse, and a single kettle flickers on and nudges the whole rhythm.
