## Scene 2: Where gas comes from  (28.0 s)

**Narration**

Natural gas starts with life. Millions of years ago, tiny plants and animals were buried under mud and rock. Heat and pressure slowly turned all of it into gas. It's trapped in rock, often a mile or more down. Some is locked in a tight rock called shale. To free it, crews may use fracking: pumping water and sand down to crack the rock. A well brings the gas up to the wellhead, a set of valves at the surface.

_Spoken as:_ "To free it, crews may use fracking. Pumping water and sand down, to crack the rock."

**Visual:** Ancient sea with tiny drifting plants/animals (label) that sink to the seabed. Sediment layers stack on top ("Millions of years"). Pressure arrows from above and a heat glow from below turn the buried layer into gas bubbles. Cross-fade to a geologic cross-section: gas in a rock pocket ("Gas trapped in rock"), a "A mile or more" depth bracket, the shale layer highlighted ("Shale"). A well is drilled down and then sideways along the shale; blue water/sand flows in and cracks spread ("Fracking: water and sand crack the shale"). Gas flows up to a red wellhead that pops in at the surface ("Wellhead").

## Scene 3: Gathering lines  (11.3 s)

**Narration**

A gas field can have many wells. Small pipes called gathering lines connect them, like streams joining a river. They carry the raw gas to a processing plant.

**Visual:** Map view of a gas field: wells pop in one by one ("Wells"). Small pipes grow from each well, merge like a river system ("Gathering lines") and run to a "Processing plant" tile.

## Scene 4: The processing plant  (13.0 s)

**Narration**

The plant strips out water and impurities. It also removes propane and butane, which are sold separately. What's left is mostly methane. And methane has no smell at all.

**Visual:** Side view of a processing plant. Raw gas enters as a mix of colored dots ("Raw gas: a messy mix"). ## Scene 6: Keeping the gas moving  (22.1 s)

**Narration**

Friction against the pipe walls slowly drains that pressure. So every 40 to 100 miles or so, a compressor station squeezes it back up. The gas moves at about the speed of a bicycle. Yellow posts along roads and fields mark roughly where a line runs, and long, cleared strips of land keep the path open for repairs.

_Spoken as:_ "So every forty to a hundred miles or so, a compressor station squeezes it back up."

## Scene 7: Saving it for winter  (13.6 s)

**Narration**

Homes use far more gas in winter. So in summer, extra gas is stored deep underground, in old, emptied gas fields, or in huge salt caverns. In winter, it flows back out.

**Visual:** A month-by-month home gas-use bar chart (tall winter bars). Cross-section with an old depleted gas field (porous dome under cap rock) and a salt cavern in a salt dome. Summer (sun, "Summer: fill up"): gas flows down and the storage fills. Winter (snowflake, "Winter: draw out"): arrows reverse and the level drops.

## Scene 8: The city gate  (21.3 s)

**Narration**

Near a town, the pipeline reaches a city gate, where your local gas company takes over. Regulators make the first big drop in pressure. Gas gets cold as it expands, so it's often warmed first. It's measured, and a smelly chemical called mercaptan is added: the rotten-egg smell, so a leak can't hide.

## Scene 11: At your house  (25.0 s)

**Narration**

At the house, the line rises to the gas meter. First, a shutoff valve. Then the regulator: the bell-shaped part. It drops the pressure to about a quarter of a pound per square inch, less push than gently blowing through a straw. The meter counts how much gas you use. Inside, pipes branch to the furnace, water heater, stove, and dryer, each with its own shutoff valve.

_Spoken as:_ "Then the regulator. The bell-shaped part."
