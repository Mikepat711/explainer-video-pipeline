---
title: How a Bike Derailleur Shifts Gears
subtitle: A cable, a spring and a slanted parallelogram
---

## [ratio] One pedal turn, two distances
The front chainring has 50 teeth. With the chain on a 25-tooth rear cog, one turn of the pedals turns the wheel twice. Shift to an 11-tooth cog, and the same pedal turn spins the wheel about four and a half times. Small cogs are fast but hard to push; big cogs are slow but easy.
> concept: The gear ratio is chainring teeth divided by cog teeth, and it sets how far one pedal turn moves the bike.
> visual: Side view of a 50-tooth chainring driving a cog fixed to the rear wheel hub, both drawn to scale by tooth count. A readout of wheel turns per pedal turn climbs from 2.0 to 4.5 as the chain moves from the 25-tooth to the 11-tooth cog, and the wheel visibly speeds up while the pedals keep the same pace.

## [shift] A cable, a spring and a parallelogram
Seen from behind, the rear cogs form a stepped cone. The derailleur hangs beside them on a hinged parallelogram that carries the chain on a small jockey wheel. Pull the shifter cable and the parallelogram swings inward, steering the chain onto a bigger cog. Let the cable go, and a return spring pushes it back toward the small cogs. The parallelogram is slanted, so the jockey wheel drops lower as the cogs get bigger.
> concept: Cable tension pushes the derailleur toward bigger cogs, a spring returns it, and the slanted linkage keeps the jockey wheel just under each cog.
> visual: Rear view: the cassette as a stepped stack of cogs, the parallelogram's two links swinging together, the jockey wheel sliding under one cog after another with the chain rising to meet it. Arrows show cable pull one way and spring return the other; a dashed line reveals the slanted track that follows the cone.

## [slack] Taking up the slack
A big cog needs more chain wrapped around it than a small one. Going from 11 to 25 teeth uses about seven more links, roughly 9 centimeters of chain. The derailleur's lower arm is spring-loaded, so it swings forward to give up that chain, then swings back to take the slack in again. That keeps the chain tight in every gear.
> concept: The sprung cage arm stores spare chain, swinging forward on big cogs and back on small ones so the chain never goes slack.
> visual: Side view of chainring, cassette and the cage arm with its tension pulley. As the chain climbs to the big cog the arm swings forward and a spare-chain readout counts up to about 9 cm; shifting back, the arm swings rearward and reels the slack in.
